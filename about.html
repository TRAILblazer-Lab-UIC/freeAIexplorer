About Homegrown AI

University of Illinois
Chicago

Homegrown AI is an AI-assisted experiment by Jeremy Riel, PhD, from the UIC TRAILblazer Lab. The name reflects the tools it collects. Many are open and community built. Many run on your school's own devices. Student work stays close to home. Learn more about the lab at trailblazerlab.org.

The site helps K-12 teachers of every subject find AI and computing tools for class. It does not list every AI tool for education. It focuses on tools that are open access and local. These tools may suit some schools' needs well, especially schools that need to keep student data in house. Teachers can use them to help students build AI and computing skills in any subject. They can also use AI to support learning in their own subject. Many of these tools keep student data inside the school. Some run with no internet at all. Others are paper activities with no device.

Why an AI-assisted experiment?

New learning tools and technologies appear every year. Many others disappear just as fast. The sheer number makes it very hard for any educator to keep up.

A rigorous AI-assisted process can help. It can scan thousands of technologies, tools, and apps. It can sort them with clear rules and check its own work. That helps educators keep a pulse on tools for teaching computing, AI, and information literacy skills.

This experiment aims to help educators make sense of the noise and the constant change. AI-written content is not always perfect. It can still be an excellent starting point.

A note on AI-generated content

Generative AI helped research, sort, and describe the tools on this site. AI can make mistakes. Some facts may be wrong or out of date. Prices, privacy terms, and features change often. Please check every detail with the provider before you use a tool with students. Nothing on this site is legal advice. Your district's privacy rules come first.

How we built it

The project ran in three stages.

  1. Research. AI research agents searched the web in four rounds. They looked for software, hardware, kits, lessons, and unplugged activities. Each item got a fact sheet with cited sources. We stopped searching once new searches began producing diminishing returns.
  2. Classification. Python rules sorted each item by facts such as cost, internet need, and where student data goes. AI agents scored judgment calls such as ease of use. A second AI coder scored a random sample on its own. We measured how often the two agreed and tightened the rules until agreement passed our target.
  3. Writing. AI agents wrote each tool page from the fact sheets. A style checker reviewed every page.

Python scripts refereed the whole research and classification process. They listed every tool up front and tracked each one through every step. They checked each result against the rules before it counted. That made sure every tool was considered on its own. The process never relied on an AI's memory to follow the research steps.

What the privacy labels mean

  • Stays on the device. The tool runs on the computer, tablet, or robot. Student work stays there.
  • Stays on a school server. The school runs the tool on its own server.
  • Local with optional cloud or accounts. The main features run locally. Some extras use a company's servers.
  • Sends student data to a company. Student work or account details go to an outside company.
  • No device needed. The activity uses paper, cards, or movement.
  • Unverified. The materials do not say which tools or accounts students need. Check before class.

What is not here

We did not test each tool in a classroom. We did not measure learning gains. Few of these tools have strong research behind them. The classroom evidence label on each page shows what we found.

Privacy on this site

This site loads nothing from other companies. It has no ads, trackers, or analytics. My List lives only in your own browser. Links to tool websites leave this site.
