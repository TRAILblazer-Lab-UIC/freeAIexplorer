AI in Every Subject

AI is part of how people read, write, create, and decide. It shows up in search results, photo apps, translation tools, and news feeds. Students meet it in every subject. That makes AI a topic for every teacher.

You do not need to teach computer science to help. Every teacher can give students experiences with AI that build critical thinking, responsible use, and creativity. This page explains why and shows where to start.

Why AI and computing belong in every subject

  • AI already touches every field. Historians use it to read old documents. Scientists use it to spot patterns in data. Artists and musicians use it to sketch ideas.
  • Students learn best in context. A lesson about bias in a history class sticks better than a lecture about bias in general.
  • Every subject has its own questions about AI. A writer asks who owns a story. A scientist asks whether the data is good. A citizen asks who decides what we see.
  • Students will use AI with or without guidance. Teachers can help them use it well.

What every teacher can build

These skills fit any class. None of them need technical training.

  1. Critical thinking. Students check AI output against sources. They spot errors, gaps, and weak reasons.
  2. Responsible use. Students learn when AI help is allowed and when it is not. They keep personal details private. They give credit for AI help.
  3. Creativity. Students do their own thinking first. AI can then help them test or extend an idea. The ideas and choices stay theirs.
  4. Data sense. Students learn that AI learns from examples that people chose. They ask what is missing from those examples.
  5. Fairness and impact. Students discuss who benefits from an AI tool and who might be harmed.

Every class can stay itself

Most lessons need no programming at all. Many are paper activities, games, or short discussions. Some tools let students train a small AI model by showing it examples. Others let a teacher run a demonstration on one computer. The goal is understanding and good judgment.

One idea for each subject

Each idea below uses a tool from this site that keeps student work on school devices or on paper. Select a tool to read its full page.

  • English and language arts. Fill in a "Large Language MadLibs" sheet and notice how word choices change a story. Uses AI Unplugged (Northwestern).
  • Math. Plot two variables on a scatter plot and describe the pattern a model might learn. Uses Orange Data Mining.
  • Science. Play a wildlife scientist and group animals by the features you observe, the way a machine learning model sorts examples. Uses Machine Learning Unplugged (The Tech Interactive).
  • Social studies. Complete the "Datasets and Data Brokers" activity and discuss what a data profile reveals about a person. Uses AI Unplugged (Northwestern).
  • Visual arts. Watch a teacher-led demo that makes an image in the style of a painter the class studied. Then compare it with the real work. Uses Stable Diffusion on a teacher computer.
  • Music. Separate a song into parts and listen to the drums alone to study the beat. Uses Audacity OpenVINO AI plugins.
  • Theater and media arts. Record a short news report with a custom background behind the anchor. Uses OBS Background Removal plugin.
  • Computer science. Run a small AI chat model on a lab computer and compare its answers with a classmate's. Uses Ollama.
  • Career and technical education. Program a robot arm to sort items by color, like a factory line. Uses Dobot Magician.
  • Engineering. Wire a sensor and a light to a small board and make the light respond. Uses ESP32 development boards.
  • World languages. Translate a paragraph you wrote and check the result word by word. Uses TranslateLocally.
  • Health and PE. Wear a micro:bit during recess and describe your most active moment from the graph. Uses micro:bit Tracking our physical activity.
  • Special education and accessibility. Create audio versions of class notes for students who prefer to listen. Uses Piper TTS.
  • Library and media. Scan a page from a printed book and compare the scanned text with the original. Uses Umi-OCR.

How to start

  1. Pick one idea for one lesson. Small steps work best.
  2. Try a paper activity first. It works without devices, accounts, or setup.
  3. Ask students what they think AI is before you begin. Return to that question at the end.
  4. Talk about what the AI got wrong. Mistakes teach as much as successes.
  5. Find more tools for your subject in the tool finder.
