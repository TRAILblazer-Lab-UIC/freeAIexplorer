Why AI That Stays in School?

Students will meet AI at school, at home, and at work. Many schools and teachers now choose to teach about it. That teaching works best with care. Students can learn about AI safely, responsibly, and productively. Good lessons protect and grow students' own creativity and critical thinking. AI should support that thinking. It should never stand in for it.

The tools a school picks shape those lessons. This page explains one option in plain terms. No technical background is needed.

The big idea behind this site

You do not need an outside AI company to teach AI or to use it. Students can explore how AI works with paper, cards, and games. They can train their own models in a web browser. They can run open AI models on school laptops, small boards, and robots. A school can also host AI tools on its own server.

The Local AI Field Guide gathers these options in one place. Each tool shows where it runs and where student data goes. Teachers can then choose with clear eyes.

Two kinds of AI

Here is a simple way to picture it. Cloud AI is like mailing a letter to a company far away. Local AI is like a calculator on your desk.

Most people meet AI through a chat website or app. Those tools run on a company's computers in a distant data center. Every word a student types travels there. The company decides how long to keep it and what to do with it.

A second kind of AI runs on devices your school already owns. It can run on a laptop, a tablet, a small robot, or a school server. Student work can stay in the building. Many of these tools are free, and many work with no internet at all.

Each approach has tradeoffs. The biggest company models can do more. Local models are smaller. They still handle many classroom tasks well. They are also easier to explain to students and to parents.

Words you will hear

  • AI model. The trained "brain" of an AI tool. It turns your words, images, or sounds into an answer.
  • Frontier model. One of the largest and newest AI models from big companies. These run only in company data centers.
  • Closed model. A model that only its company can see and run. You use it through the company's website or app.
  • Open model. A model whose files anyone can download and run. People also call these "open-weight" models. Examples include Llama, Gemma, and Mistral.
  • Open source. Software with public code that anyone can read, check, and change.
  • Cloud AI. AI that runs on a company's servers over the internet.
  • Local AI. AI that runs on the device in front of you. People also call this "on-device" AI.
  • On-premises AI. AI that runs on a server your school or district owns and controls.
  • Data center. A large building full of computers that run cloud services.

One name causes confusion. OpenAI is a company. Its best-known product, ChatGPT, runs on closed models in company data centers. The company name does not make a tool open or local.

Free means many things

The word "free" can hide very different deals. It helps to ask which kind of free a tool offers.

  • Free of cost. You pay no money. This is the most common meaning.
  • Free to use as you wish. Open-source tools give you the right to run, study, change, and share them. People call this "free as in freedom."
  • Free of data collection. Some tools collect nothing about students. Others collect a lot and still cost nothing.
  • Free of accounts. Some tools need no sign-in at all. No account means no student profile on a company server.
  • Free for now. Some tools offer a free tier or trial. The terms can change, and features can move behind a paywall later.
  • Free because you are the product. Some services earn money from ads or from the data users create. The price is paid in information.

A tool can be free in one way and not another. A chat service may cost nothing and still keep every prompt. An open-source program may be free to use and still need a costly computer to run. This site labels cost, privacy, accounts, and open source on their own. That way each kind of free stays visible.

Free of cost rarely means free of effort. Someone still installs the tool, updates it, and supports teachers. The setup and IT ratings on each tool page help with that planning.

Control brings safety and security

Running AI on computers your school controls has value beyond privacy.

  • Data stays home. Student work never reaches an outside server. A breach at a vendor cannot expose it.
  • Fewer outside connections. Each cloud service opens another path for data to leave. Local tools shrink that list.
  • Rules stay steady. A school picks a tool version and keeps it. A vendor cannot change features, terms, or prices overnight.
  • Content can be checked. Teachers and IT staff can test a local model before students use it. They can add filters and choose safer models.
  • Work goes on during outages. Local tools keep running through internet outages, vendor shutdowns, and service limits.
  • Code can be inspected. Open-source tools can be reviewed by IT staff and outside experts.
  • Clear responsibility. The school knows who manages the tool and where its data lives. That makes privacy reviews simpler.

Control also brings duties. Local tools need updates and safe settings. Some ship with default passwords or with usage reporting turned on. A school-run server needs care, like any other school system. The checklists on each tool page point out these steps.

Concerns with big AI services in schools

Big AI chat services can be useful. They also raise questions that schools should weigh.

  • Student privacy. Laws such as FERPA and COPPA protect student records and children's data. Many AI services keep what users type. Some use it to train future models.
  • Age limits. Many AI services require users to be 13 or older. Some require 18. Younger students are left out.
  • Cost. Free tiers change often. School plans can cost thousands of dollars each year.
  • Internet. Cloud tools stop working on a weak connection. Many rural schools face this every day.
  • Changing terms. A company can change its rules at any time. A tool that fits district policy today may not fit next year.
  • Hidden workings. Students type a question and get an answer. They rarely see how the AI reached it.

What this site looks for

The Local AI Field Guide looks for tools that keep students and schools in control. The best fits share some traits.

  • They run on school devices or a school server.
  • They work with little or no internet.
  • They cost nothing or very little.
  • They keep student work inside the school.
  • Many are open source.

The site also lists paid tools and cloud tools. Each one carries a clear privacy label. You decide what fits your school.

Open source in plain words

Open-source software comes with its code in public view. Anyone can read it, check it, and improve it. Most open-source tools cost nothing to download. Large groups of volunteers and organizations keep many of these projects going.

Open source matters for schools in three ways.

  1. Schools can see what the software does. Privacy experts can check the code.
  2. The software stays available. A company cannot take it away.
  3. License costs drop to zero. Budgets go further.

Open hardware works the same way. The designs for a board or robot are public. The BBC micro:bit and the Arduino are well-known examples.

What local AI means

Local AI runs on a device in the room. The model lives on that computer, tablet, or small board. Its work stays on the device.

Small AI models have grown much better in recent years. A normal laptop can now run a helpful chat model. A small camera board can recognize faces, objects, and colors. A web browser can train an image model in minutes.

Here are some examples you will find on this site.

  • Teachable Machine trains image, sound, and pose models inside the web browser.
  • Ollama runs open AI chat models on a school computer.
  • HuskyLens is a small AI camera that recognizes objects with no internet.
  • Whisper.cpp turns speech into text on the device.

Energy and water

Big AI services run in data centers. Data centers use large amounts of electricity. Many also use water to keep their machines cool. Researchers are still measuring the full size of this footprint.

Local AI works differently. A small model runs on a laptop that the school already owns. It uses about the same power as other work on that laptop. It adds no data center cooling water. Paper activities use no electricity at all.

Every device still takes energy and materials to build. Local AI is lighter. It still carries some cost. Using the devices you already have is a good place to start.

Teaching about AI with care

AI shapes the news students read and the jobs they will hold. Students benefit from understanding it as citizens and creators. The AI4K12 initiative names five big ideas for K-12 students.

  1. Computers sense the world through sensors.
  2. AI systems keep models of the world and use them to reason.
  3. Computers can learn from data.
  4. AI agents interact with people in natural ways.
  5. AI can help or harm society.

Hands-on tools make these ideas visible. Students train a model and watch it improve. They test it and find its mistakes. They learn that people chose the data behind each model. That builds healthy skepticism and real skill.

Thoughtful AI lessons keep students in the driver's seat.

  • Students do the thinking first. AI can come in later to test or extend their ideas.
  • Students question AI output. They check facts, spot errors, and explain why an answer is weak.
  • Students make their own work. AI can help them explore, but the ideas and choices stay theirs.
  • Students learn the limits. AI can be wrong, biased, or overconfident.
  • Students practice safe habits. They keep personal details private and use AI only where teachers allow it.

What you can do next

  1. Start with one tool. Press the "For AI Beginners" button on the explorer.
  2. Try a paper activity first. Unplugged lessons need no setup and no accounts.
  3. Talk with your IT team early. Share the privacy label and the setup steps.
  4. Save tools to My List. Print a "Try It Out" plan for your team.
  5. Share what works with other teachers.

A word of caution

No tool is perfect. Local tools can still have bugs. Some "local" tools turn on cloud features by default. Some collect usage data unless you turn it off. Read the privacy section on each tool page.
