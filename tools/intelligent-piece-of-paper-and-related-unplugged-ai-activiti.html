Intelligent Piece of Paper and related unplugged AI activities

Three device-free AI activities described in an ISTE+ASCD blog post.

AIUnpluggedGood for AI beginners

Overview

This is a blog post by Nicole Krueger on the ISTE+ASCD blog. It was updated on July 20, 2022 from an October 2018 post. It describes three device-free activities. In The Intelligent Piece of Paper, students play tic tac toe against a paper that holds instructions. In The Turing Test, volunteers act as a computer and a human. In How Facial Recognition Works, students break images into feature lists.

Benefits

For students

  • Students see that an algorithm is a set of clear rules.
  • Students try to tell a machine from a human by its answers.
  • Students learn how a computer might match a face by its features.

For teachers

  • The post links to ready materials for each activity.
  • The three activities are short and use common supplies.
  • Teachers can pick one activity or run all three.

For schools

  • The post and the linked materials are free.
  • The activities need no devices or accounts.
  • The activities collect no student data.

Getting started checklist

Where is the AI in this product?

The activities have no running AI program. They teach how algorithms, the Turing test, and facial recognition work. Students act out each idea with paper and role play. They build skills in following rules and in listing features.

Privacy and safety

The activities run on paper, and no account is needed. The activities collect no student data. The post links to outside pages, and the vendor does not describe those sites. A teacher should check the linked materials before class.

How this can be used for Computer Science
  1. Play tic tac toe against the paper and follow its rules exactly.
  2. Write a better rule sheet and test whether it can win.
  3. Act as the computer or the human in a Turing test and see if the class can tell which is which.
  4. Break a character image into a list of features and let a partner guess the character.
  5. Explain how a facial recognition tool might get a match wrong.
