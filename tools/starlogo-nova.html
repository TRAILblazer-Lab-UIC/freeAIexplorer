StarLogo Nova

A free browser tool for building games and simulations with blocks.

FREEComputingSends student data to a company

Overview

StarLogo Nova is an agent-based game and simulation environment from the MIT Scheller Teacher Education Program. It uses a blocks-based language and a 3D view that runs in the browser. It suits upper elementary through high school. Students program many small agents and watch what the group does together.

Benefits

For students

  • Students see how simple rules for each agent produce larger patterns.
  • Students build their own games and simulations with blocks.
  • Students can share projects in public galleries and work with others.

For teachers

  • The tool is free and needs no installation.
  • A published study covered 369 students, and the tool was used in five high school biology units.
  • Teachers can show ideas such as genetics through a model that students can change.

For schools

  • The tool costs nothing and works on Chromebooks and older computers.
  • Student projects live on the vendor's servers and the connection uses HTTPS.
  • IT work is small, but the privacy policy should be reviewed first.

Getting started checklist

Where is the AI in this product?

StarLogo Nova has no AI. Students program agents with blocks and run the simulation in the browser. This builds the skill of modeling, which means choosing rules and checking what data results.

Privacy and safety

The internet is needed at all times and projects are stored on the vendor's servers. The policy summary says IP addresses and an optional email address are collected, and cookies store session information. It says collected information may be used for research on how people use the tool. The research could not read the policy page in full and did not confirm whether accounts are required. A teacher should read the privacy policy and decide whether students need an account.

How this can be used for Science
  1. Explore a genetics simulation from a biology unit and describe how traits spread through the agents.
  2. Change one rule in a population model and record how the results shift.
  3. Build a simple model of a disease spreading through a crowd and graph the outcome.
  4. Compare two runs of the same simulation and explain why they differ.
  5. Present a model to the class and name its limits.
How this can be used for Computer Science
  1. Snap together blocks that tell an agent how to move and run the simulation.
  2. Write a rule that makes agents change color after they touch each other.
  3. Build a short game with a goal and a score.
  4. Debug a simulation that does not behave as expected by reading each block.
  5. Publish a project to the gallery and write a short note on how it works.
