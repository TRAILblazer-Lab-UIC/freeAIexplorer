Interactive and Safe AI for KS2 upwards (Computing at School blog)

A blog describing an offline workshop where students train image classifiers on Raspberry Pi kits.

FREEAIComputingStays on the device

Overview

This is a blog post that describes a school workshop in two halves. In the first half, student teams use a Raspberry Pi 4 and a USB camera to train an image classifier. It tells human faces from toy characters. In the second half, students make images from a limited set of prompt words. The author describes it as a presenter-led workshop and not a ready-to-run lesson pack.

Benefits

For students

  • Students train and test a real image classifier in teams.
  • Students compare accuracy with bar charts.
  • Students explore how generated images can show bias.

For teachers

  • The post shows a full session plan to copy.
  • All images are moderated before students see them.
  • The workshop ends with the deletion of all training data.

For schools

  • The post is free to read.
  • Everything runs on an isolated hotspot with no internet.
  • Each team needs a Raspberry Pi 4, USB camera, tablet, and power bank. The presenter's software is not public.

Getting started checklist

Where is the AI in this product?

The AI is an image classifier trained on a Raspberry Pi and a set of pre-made generated images. Training runs on the device and images come from a school laptop. Students capture photos, train, test, and compare results across teams. They can then ask why two classifiers differ.

Privacy and safety

No account is needed, and the setup uses an isolated hotspot with no internet. The author says the machine learning runs locally on the Pi. Accuracy results go to the presenter's laptop on the isolated network. All images and models are deleted at the end. The author notes that children under 13 cannot legally use online generative AI services, which is why the setup is offline. A teacher should check that students photograph only toys and agreed faces.

How this can be used for Computer Science
  1. Capture photos of a human face and a toy and train a classifier.
  2. Test the classifier on new photos and record its accuracy.
  3. Compare your team's bar chart with another team's chart.
  4. Add more photos and see how the accuracy changes.
  5. Write prompts from a limited word list and discuss bias in the images.
