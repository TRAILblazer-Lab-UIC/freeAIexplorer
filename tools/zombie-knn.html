Zombie KNN

An unplugged lesson that teaches K-nearest neighbors through a zombie survival map.

AIUnpluggedGood for AI beginners

Overview

Zombie KNN teaches the K-nearest neighbors algorithm through a zombie survival scenario. Students predict safe parts of a town from data. It needs no computers or prior machine learning knowledge. The lesson fits one 45 minute class for middle school and also suits introductory high school.

Benefits

For students

  • Students take averages to predict zombie counts.
  • Students check how accurate their predictions are.
  • Students learn how nearby data points guide a prediction.

For teachers

  • The download has a lesson plan, slides, two activity guides and answer keys.
  • Code.org uses it as Lesson 11 in a unit on AI and machine learning.
  • It fits in one 45 minute class.

For schools

  • The materials are free to download.
  • The lesson is on paper and few devices are needed.
  • No accounts are needed.

Getting started checklist

Where is the AI in this product?

The lesson teaches K-nearest neighbors, a basic machine learning method. It runs on paper with no computer. Students use training data from a map to predict a new value. They then measure accuracy with numbers.

Privacy and safety

The lesson is on paper and no student data is collected. No accounts are needed. A teacher should review the zombie theme for the age of the class. Some students may find the topic upsetting.

How this can be used for Computer Science
  1. Predict the zombie count for a town square using its nearest neighbors on the map.
  2. Change the number of neighbors and see how the prediction moves.
  3. Explain what training data means using the zombie map.
  4. Compare your predictions with the answer key.
  5. Describe how a real system could use nearest neighbors to guess a value.
How this can be used for Math
  1. Take the average of nearby values to make a prediction.
  2. Compute the difference between a prediction and the true value.
  3. Find the average error over all predictions.
  4. Graph predictions against the real counts.
  5. Explain whether a higher or lower accuracy score is better.
