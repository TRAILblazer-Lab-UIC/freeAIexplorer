NSTA Insight Maker user to creator modeling lesson

A high school lesson where students move from using models to building them.

FREEComputingUnverified

The privacy details for this item are unverified. Check which tools and accounts students will use before class.

Overview

This Science Teacher article describes four 55 minute sessions in a high school STEM elective. Students first use a Concord Consortium Earth energy balance simulation and sketch conceptual models. They then learn stocks, flows, and variables in Insight Maker and build a bathtub model. In the last two days they build a wolf and moose predator prey model from Isle Royale data.

Benefits

For students

  • Students move from using a model to building one.
  • Students work with real wolf and moose data.
  • Students learn stocks, flows, and variables.

For teachers

  • The lesson lists links to NGSS HS-ESS3-6 and HS-LS2-5.
  • The article gives a clear four session plan.
  • The authors note that Insight Maker does not fix syntax errors, which students must debug.

For schools

  • The lesson is free.
  • It needs one computer per student or pair.
  • Students need Insight Maker accounts.

Getting started checklist

Where is the AI in this product?

This lesson has no AI. It builds skills in modeling, variables, and debugging. Students learn how a model can predict change over time.

Privacy and safety

Accounts are needed, and the internet is needed at all times. Insight Maker models can be private or public. The research found a privacy policy from the parent company but did not open it. Its link to Insight Maker is unconfirmed. The materials do not say what data Insight Maker collects. A teacher should check the data practices and set models to private before students sign up.

How this can be used for Science
  1. Run the energy balance simulation and sketch a model of what you see.
  2. Build the wolf and moose model with Isle Royale data.
  3. Change the moose growth rate and describe the effect.
  4. Compare the model output to the real data.
  5. Write a claim about what drives the populations.
How this can be used for Math
  1. Build a bathtub model with a stock and two flows.
  2. Explain how a flow changes a stock over time.
  3. Write an equation for a variable in the model.
  4. Graph the stock and describe its shape.
  5. Fix a function that returns the wrong result.
