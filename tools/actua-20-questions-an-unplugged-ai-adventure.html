Actua 20 Questions: An Unplugged AI Adventure

A one-hour unplugged activity that builds a decision tree to classify Arctic animals.

AIUnpluggedGood for AI beginners

Overview

This is a one-hour in-person activity from Actua for grades 2 to 6, ages 7 to 12. Participants play a rock paper scissors game with dice to see how AI makes decisions. They then build a decision tree with animal cards to classify Arctic animals. The facilitator uses a laptop, projector, and speakers for a slide deck.

Benefits

For students

  • Students see how a machine can make choices with rules.
  • Students build a decision tree with real animal features.
  • Students reflect on safe and ethical AI use.

For teachers

  • The write-up covers a game, a tree activity, and a debrief in one hour.
  • It adds extensions and delivery adaptations.
  • Learning goals include algorithms and algorithmic thinking.

For schools

  • The activity is free to download.
  • It needs only paper, dice, chart paper, markers, and a projector.
  • Students need no accounts or devices.

Getting started checklist

Where is the AI in this product?

This activity has no software and no AI runs on any device. Students act out how AI makes decisions with dice and then build a decision tree on paper. They build skills in classification and algorithmic thinking. The slide deck covers ethical and safe use of AI.

Privacy and safety

Students work with dice and paper cards and no account is needed. The write-up describes no student data collection. The facilitator uses a laptop and projector to show a slide deck. The internet is needed to download the materials. A teacher should check the slide deck before class.

How this can be used for Computer Science
  1. Play Rock, Paper, Decisions with dice and describe the rule the game follows.
  2. Build a decision tree that sorts the animal cards into groups.
  3. Test the tree on a new animal card and see where it lands.
  4. Change one question in the tree and watch how the groups change.
  5. Discuss one way AI use could be unsafe or unfair.
How this can be used for Science
  1. Sort Arctic animals by features such as fur, flippers, and wings.
  2. Use a question tree to identify a mystery animal in 20 questions.
  3. Compare two animals and list the features that set them apart.
  4. Draw the tree for a new animal group of the class's choice.
  5. Explain why some features help sort animals better than others.
