The PocketLab

Wireless science sensors that send live data to a phone or tablet app.

FREEComputingLocal with optional cloud or accounts

Overview

PocketLab sells wireless data sensors for classroom experiments, along with a free app and a web-based Notebook. Students run experiments, collect real time data, and analyze it. The Notebook offers ready-made lessons and teacher and student accounts. The company also lists premium lessons from partners such as OpenSciEd and Amoeba Sisters and free teacher professional development.

Benefits

For students

  • Students collect real data from their own experiments.
  • Students see live graphs on a phone or tablet.
  • Students analyze results in the Notebook.

For teachers

  • The Notebook offers ready-made lessons.
  • Google Classroom and ClassLink account integrations are available.
  • Free professional development is offered for teachers.

For schools

  • The app and basic Notebook are free. Sensor prices are not listed and the site directs visitors to request a quote.
  • Students need a phone or tablet for the app.
  • IEEE Spectrum describes using it on a cart for acceleration and momentum.

Getting started checklist

Where is the AI in this product?

The vendor does not describe any AI. The sensors measure physical motion and the app graphs the results. Students practice collecting data and reading graphs.

Privacy and safety

Students can use the app and connect sensors without an account. The Notebook stores student usernames, avatars, class codes, and email for ClassLink users. Data goes to the vendor's cloud. The policy says students under 13 need parental or guardian consent for a Notebook student account. This is a consent rule and not a minimum age. The policy does not mention FERPA and the site uses Facebook, Google, and Twitter tracking tools for visitors. Teachers create the accounts. Use aliases for student names and review the privacy policy.

How this can be used for Science
  1. Attach a sensor to a cart on a track and record its acceleration, as IEEE Spectrum describes.
  2. Compare data from two runs of the same experiment.
  3. Graph the motion and describe it in words.
  4. Predict a result and test it with the sensor.
  5. Share a Notebook page with a conclusion.
How this can be used for Engineering and Design
  1. Change a cart's setup and measure the effect on momentum.
  2. Design a test that checks one variable at a time.
  3. Fix a test whose data looks wrong.
  4. Build a simple device and measure how it moves.
  5. Present the design changes you made to improve results.
