Hexapawn: Introduction to Machine Learning

A 30-minute paper game where students train a bead-driven hexapawn computer.

AIUnpluggedGood for AI beginners

Overview

This activity is suggested for age 11 and up and takes about 30 minutes. Students play yellow on a 3x3 board against a paper hexapawn computer. On each computer turn they draw a bead from a bag to pick a move from a diagram. After a computer loss, they cross off its last move. Over many games the computer stops repeating its mistakes.

Benefits

For students

  • Students see a machine learn from its losses.
  • Students play a simple game and watch the computer improve.
  • Students think about strategy and move counting.

For teachers

  • The page offers two PDFs, a game board with a score table and a Computer Moves sheet.
  • The activity fits into one short lesson.
  • Teachers need no AI background.

For schools

  • The PDFs are free.
  • You supply a board, chips, a bag, and beads.
  • No devices or accounts are needed.

Getting started checklist

Where is the AI in this product?

The activity has no running AI program. The paper computer shows reinforcement learning, in which a machine learns from losing moves. Students cross off each losing move so the computer avoids it next time. They see learning happen on paper over many games.

Privacy and safety

The activity runs on paper and no account is needed. The materials describe no student data collection, and the research did not read a privacy policy. The internet is used to download the PDFs. The page warns of small parts. A teacher should supervise the beads and chips. A teacher should also check the usage terms before copying the PDFs widely.

Ideas by subject

Select a subject to see five classroom ideas.

How this can be used for Computer Science
  1. Train the paper computer by playing several games and crossing off each losing move.
  2. Chart the number of computer wins over ten games and describe the trend.
  3. Explain to a partner why crossing off a move teaches the computer.
  4. Predict how many games the computer needs to become hard to beat.
  5. Compare this paper computer with a program that learns from mistakes.
How this can be used for Math
  1. Count the possible first moves on the 3x3 board.
  2. Find a winning strategy for the human player and test it.
  3. Record the results of each game in the score table.
  4. Work out the share of games the computer wins before and after training.
  5. Draw all the positions the computer could face after two moves.
