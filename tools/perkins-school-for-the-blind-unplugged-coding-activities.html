Perkins School for the Blind unplugged coding activities

Tactile sequencing activities for students with visual impairments, using cards and cups.

ComputingUnplugged

Overview

This Perkins blog post describes unplugged sequencing activities for students with visual impairments. In one activity, students write arrow card moves that let a student robot rebuild a cup stack from a tactile card. In another, students build a route on a tactile grid page with Wikki Stix. The post is a blog article and not a packaged lesson.

Benefits

For students

  • Students touch every card and tile, which makes sequencing concrete.
  • Students practice giving exact commands to a partner who acts as the robot.
  • Students prepare for digital coding tools through hands-on practice.

For teachers

  • The post explains the steps in plain language.
  • Arrow command cards can be made tactile with materials a teacher already has.
  • The post points to related Perkins pages on Sphero and Dash for the next step.

For schools

  • The post is free to read.
  • Cups, cards and Wikki Stix are ordinary classroom supplies.
  • The activities need no internet and no IT help. Students use no devices.

Getting started checklist

Where is the AI in this product?

These activities have no AI. They build an AI-related skill, which is sequencing. Students plan an ordered list of commands and watch a robot partner follow them.

Privacy and safety

The activities run on paper and classroom supplies. No accounts are needed and no student data is collected. Nothing here needs the internet during class. The research read the post only through search snippets. A teacher should check that cups and cards are safe and comfortable for each student's needs.

How this can be used for Computer Science
  1. Write arrow card moves that tell a student robot how to rebuild a cup stack.
  2. Build a route on a tactile grid with Wikki Stix and write the forward commands.
  3. Swap command lists with a partner and follow them exactly.
  4. Find a mistake in a command list and fix it.
  5. Compare the paper activity with a robot such as Dash and talk about what is the same.
How this can be used for Special Education and Accessibility
  1. Feel a tactile card of a cup stack and describe it aloud to a partner.
  2. Make your own arrow cards with raised marks so each direction feels different.
  3. Trace a Wikki Stix route with a finger before writing the commands.
  4. Act as the robot and follow a classmate's commands one step at a time.
  5. Choose which tactile tools help most and tell a teacher why.
