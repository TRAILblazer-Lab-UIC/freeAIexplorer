## Overview
Brain in a Bag is a group activity by Peter McOwan and Paul Curzon of cs4fn. Each student plays a neuron. Students pass a tube along a rope to send a spike to a downstream neuron. Neurons that need several inputs to fire act like AND gates. The group builds an artificial brain that plays a simple game of snap.

## Benefits for students
- Students act out how a neural network passes signals.
- Students see how AND gates link to neurons.
- Students build a network that plays snap.

## Benefits for teachers
- The activity is on STEM Learning and on the cs4fn site.
- A related cs4fn web applet builds a brain that plays red-black snap.
- The materials are simple props.

## Benefits for schools
- The activity is listed as free to download, though the STEM Learning page could not be read.
- It needs no computers.
- It is available in English and Russian.

## Where is the AI?
The activity has no software and no AI runs on any device. Students act as neurons in a neural network and send spikes to each other. The firing rules work like AND gates in logic circuits. Students build skills in logic and in seeing how simple parts form a network.

## Privacy and safety
The activity uses physical props and no devices, and it collects no student data. The research could not read the official pages and did not confirm whether the download needs a login. The internet is needed only to get the materials. A teacher should check the activity page before class. Both official pages returned errors during research. A 2024 paper suggests a single simulated neuron is a better entry point for middle school.

## Ideas by subject
### computer_science
1. Play a neuron and pass a tube only when two inputs have arrived.
2. Draw the group's network and mark each AND gate.
3. Change a neuron's rule and see how the snap game changes.
4. Use the cs4fn applet to build a brain that plays red-black snap.
5. Explain in a short talk how many simple neurons can make a decision.

### science
1. Describe how a real neuron sends a spike to the next neuron.
2. Compare the class model with a drawing of a real nerve cell.
3. Test how long a spike takes to travel along the class line.
4. Explain why a neuron might wait for two signals before it fires.
5. List one way the class model is different from a real brain.
