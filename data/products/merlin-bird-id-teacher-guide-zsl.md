## Overview
This ZSL teacher guide describes a school biodiversity survey of birds with the free Merlin Bird ID app. Students visit one of six preset sample locations and record date, time, weather, temperature, and wind. They stay quiet for about 5 minutes, then record for 5 uninterrupted minutes with Merlin. They note species heard and enter the data into a spreadsheet.

## Benefits for students
- Students collect real data in a repeatable survey.
- Students sense check unexpected results.
- Students practice recording and entering data.

## Benefits for teachers
- The guide gives clear steps for each survey session.
- A paper data sheet leads to a digital spreadsheet.
- A University of Arizona lesson and a Science Scope article show similar uses.

## Benefits for schools
- The guide and the Merlin app are free.
- Merlin can run offline on school devices with regional data downloaded in advance.
- Devices need to be set up before the survey.

## Getting started
- [ ] Find the ZSL guide for birds and Merlin.

- [ ] Print the data sheet.

- [ ] Practice one 5 minute session before the full class.

## Where is the AI?
The AI is Sound ID, a model that identifies bird sounds. It runs on the device. Students record the birds they hear, read the suggestions, and then sense check them. They use the results to build their own data set.

## Privacy and safety
The materials do not state which accounts students need. The visible guide text says the app can be used offline once regional data is downloaded. The guide text did not show account setup or any data sharing with Cornell. The research did not read the Merlin privacy policy. Merlin may require an account, which a teacher should check. A teacher should confirm whether recordings are stored and who can see them.

## Ideas by subject
### science
1. Run a 5 minute survey at one location and record the species heard.
2. Compare two locations and describe the differences in species.
3. Look at weather notes and discuss how they may affect bird calls.
4. Sense check a surprising species and decide whether to keep it.
5. Share findings in a short report on school biodiversity.

### math
1. Enter survey data into a spreadsheet and total each species.
2. Make a bar chart of the species counts.
3. Find the most common species and the range of counts.
4. Compare the average count across the six locations.
5. Write a question that the data can answer and test it.
