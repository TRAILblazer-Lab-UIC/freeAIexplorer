## Overview
This is a lesson idea for Years 3 to 4 and 5 to 6. It starts with an unplugged activity where students role-play paired questions and answers to see how chatbots anticipate input. Students then explore Google Translate with text, speech, and camera input, and log successes and limits. Optional Scratch projects build a text-to-speech translator, a keyword chatbot, and a two-sprite conversation.

## Benefits for students
- Students role-play a chatbot before they use any tool.
- Students test where machine translation succeeds and fails.
- Students build their own translator or chatbot in Scratch.

## Benefits for teachers
- The lesson links to the Australian Curriculum Digital Technologies and German language codes.
- Three plugged projects run at easy, medium, and harder levels.

## Benefits for schools

## Where is the AI?
The lesson covers natural language processing, machine translation, and chatbots. Google Translate runs in Google's cloud. Students compare learning from examples with hard-coded rules, and then build a small chatbot that follows rules.

## Privacy and safety
The page does not state account needs, and the internet is needed at all times. Student text, speech and camera input go to Google when students use Translate. The research did not confirm the Scratch account needs. The lesson page does not discuss privacy, and no privacy policy was reviewed. A teacher should check the school's Google terms. A teacher should also have students use sample phrases and keep faces and names out of the camera input.

## Ideas by subject
### world_languages
1. Translate five classroom phrases with Google Translate and check them with a language guide.
2. Log where the translator is correct and where it makes mistakes.
3. Speak a sentence into Translate and note what the tool heard.
4. Build a Scratch translator that speaks a phrase in German.
5. Make a keyword chatbot that answers a greeting in another language.

### computer_science
1. Act out a chatbot in the inside and outside circles and list the questions it expects.
2. Explain how a keyword chatbot decides which reply to give.
3. Build the two-sprite conversation in Scratch.
4. Compare a rule-based chatbot with a translator that learns from examples.
5. Test the Scratch translator with a partner and fix one bug.
