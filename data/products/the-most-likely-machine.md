## Overview
The Most Likely Machine is a free browser game from the design firm Artefact for 10 to 14 year olds. Students make yearbook superlatives for historical figures such as Albert Einstein and Rosa Parks. They assign weights to traits like intelligence, popularity, creativity, or leadership. The algorithm then picks the winners.

## Benefits for students
- Students see how their own weighting choices decide who wins.
- Students read an explanation of why each winner was picked.
- Students connect the activity to real cases of algorithmic bias.

## Benefits for teachers
- The activity runs in a few minutes with no setup.
- It leads naturally into a group discussion.
- A writing or debate task can follow, such as a debate on algorithms in hiring or admissions.

## Benefits for schools
- The game is free, with no fees for classroom use.
- It runs in a browser.
- IT involvement is none.

## Where is the AI?
The game has no AI. It uses a simple weighted formula that students set themselves. It builds the skill of understanding how algorithms make decisions and how bias enters them. That skill carries over to real AI systems.

## Privacy and safety
The research found no privacy information. It did not confirm accounts or analytics because the tool site returned no readable content. The browser game needs the internet throughout. A Tech and Learning article does not discuss privacy and advises checking the platform directly. Check the privacy terms on the Artefact site. Preview the historical figures and choose the ones that suit the class.

## Ideas by subject
### computer_science
1. Set the weights for a yearbook award and list who wins.
2. Change one weight and note how the winner changes.
3. Explain in your own words why the algorithm is not neutral.
4. Connect the game to a social media feed and describe a similar bias.
5. Write a rule for a fair algorithm and test it with a partner.

### social_studies
1. Choose a historical figure and explain why a trait should count more.
2. Discuss who gets to decide the weights in a real selection process.
3. Compare winners across different weight settings and record the pattern.
4. Debate whether an algorithm should help decide hiring or admissions.
5. Write a short statement on how bias can affect people in history and today.
