## Overview
The USA Bebras Computing Challenge is a free informatics and computational thinking competition. Students ages 6 to 18 answer 15 tasks in 45 minutes. There are six age groups, from Kits at ages 6-8 to Seniors at ages 16-18. A teacher registers as a Coordinator and supervises students during the challenge window.

## Benefits for students
- Students solve logic and number puzzles in a timed challenge.
- Students are placed in an age group with easy, medium, and hard tasks.
- Students can try practice challenges on a separate site.

## Benefits for teachers
- Teachers can download scores, certificates, and an answer sheet afterward.
- Teachers can fit the challenge into lessons.
- A teacher enrolls students as a Coordinator.

## Benefits for schools
- Participation is free per the official site.
- Students need only a computer with a web browser.
- The challenge window is short. The 2026 challenge runs November 9 to 20.

## Getting started
- [ ] Find the USA Bebras site and read about the Coordinator role.
- [ ] Register as a Coordinator.
- [ ] Try the practice challenges on the separate practice site.
- [ ] Enroll your students.
- [ ] Ask IT to check that student computers can reach the site.
- [ ] Supervise students during the challenge window.
- [ ] Download scores and certificates when the window closes.

## Where is the AI?
This challenge has no AI. It builds computational thinking, including logic and number reasoning. Students work through each task and choose an answer within the time limit.

## Privacy and safety
The challenge runs in the vendor's cloud and needs the internet at all times. Coordinators register through a portal. The materials do not say whether students need their own accounts. The site links a privacy policy as a PDF and the research did not read it. The materials do not say what student data is collected. A teacher should read that policy and ask what the organizers keep before enrolling students.

## Ideas by subject
### computer_science
1. Try a practice challenge and explain how you found each answer.
2. Compare easy, medium, and hard tasks and say what makes the hard ones hard.
3. Review answers after the challenge and find the pattern in the tasks you missed.
4. Discuss which computational thinking skill each task used.
5. Make a short list of tips for a younger group taking the challenge.

### math
1. Use logic to narrow the choices on a task.
2. Solve a number reasoning task with a different method and compare.
3. Time yourself on five practice tasks and find the slowest type.
4. Find the pattern in a sequence of numbers.
5. Write your own number puzzle for a classmate.
