## Overview
This is a 45 minute Purdue 4-H lesson by Abigail Phillips for grades 4 to 7. Small groups of 2 to 4 sort unlabeled image cards of poison ivy and Virginia creeper. They first sort by patterns they find themselves and then use clues from a plant identification sheet. The lesson links this to how image recognition and machine learning improve with more labeled examples. It ends with a wrap-up discussion.

## Benefits for students
- Students invent their own sorting rules from unlabeled cards.
- Students learn real clues for telling two plants apart.
- Students see why labeled examples help a machine learn.

## Benefits for teachers
- The lesson is a short PDF with a page of discussion.
- It lists NGSS practices for data and for argument from evidence.
- It needs only printed cards and one identification sheet per group.

## Benefits for schools
- The PDF is free.
- No computers, accounts, or internet are needed.
- No student data is collected.

## Where is the AI?
The lesson has no running AI program. It models image classification, in which a machine sorts pictures into groups. Students sort cards first by their own patterns and then by labeled clues. They see why more labeled examples help a machine do better.

## Privacy and safety
The lesson runs on paper with printed cards and a sheet. The lesson text mentions no accounts and no data collection. The internet is needed only to download the PDF. The research read no privacy policy for this item. Part 2 was not read directly. The lesson uses images of poison ivy. A teacher should remind students that these plants can harm skin and tell them not to touch unknown plants.

## Ideas by subject
### science
1. Sort the unlabeled plant cards by patterns that you find yourselves.
2. Use leaflet count, edge, and shine clues to sort the cards again.
3. Compare your first sort with your second sort and tell which was more accurate.
4. Explain to a partner which clue was most helpful.
5. Argue from evidence which plant is poison ivy and which is Virginia creeper.

### computer_science
1. Describe your first sorting rule as a set of steps a machine could follow.
2. Add a labeled example and see whether your rule changes.
3. Count how many cards you sorted correctly before and after the clues.
4. Explain how more labeled examples help a machine improve.
5. Name a real tool that uses image recognition and tell what it might get wrong.
