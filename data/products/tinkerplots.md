## Overview
TinkerPlots is data visualization and modeling software for dynamic data exploration. Students build plots from data pieces and do not choose premade graph types. The site says it suits middle school through university and can teach grade 4 and up. It came from an NSF funded project and runs on Mac and Windows with a purchased license.

## Benefits for students
- Students build a plot piece by piece and see how data groups together.
- Students use probability and data modeling tools.
- Students can explore data in grade 4 and up.

## Benefits for teachers
- A 2005 article reports math teachers use it to teach data analysis aligned to NCTM standards.
- Preview mode runs until a license is bought.
- The tool originated from UMass Amherst research.

## Benefits for schools
- It needs a purchased license. The licenses are one year or perpetual, with tiers of 1 to 49, 50 to 99, and 100 or more computers.
- Per computer prices were not visible on the page.
- Mac needs macOS 10.15 or later. It is not available on Chromebooks.

## Getting started
- [ ] Visit the TinkerPlots site and read about version 3.0 and 2.3.
- [ ] Try preview mode on one computer.
- [ ] Ask the vendor for a school quote for your number of computers.
- [ ] Buy a license.
- [ ] Ask IT to install the Mac or Windows installer.
- [ ] Enter the activation code.
- [ ] Try a first data set with a small group.

## Where is the AI?
TinkerPlots has no AI. Students decide how to sort and plot data by hand. They build skills in reading data and in spotting patterns.

## Privacy and safety
No account is needed and the internet is used at setup. The privacy policy covers only the website. It collects purchase email, contact form name, email and IP address, and one simple cookie. It does not mention student data and does not describe what the software transmits. The research did not document software data transmission. Ask IT to check network activity and plan where students save their files.

## Ideas by subject
### math
1. Build a plot of class heights by grouping data pieces.
2. Compare the centers of two groups and describe the difference.
3. Make a spinner model and run it many times.
4. Explain how a plot would change with new data.
5. Present a plot and say what claim it supports.

### science
1. Collect measurements from an experiment and plot them.
2. Look for a pattern across trials.
3. Model a chance event and compare results to real data.
4. Spot an outlier and decide whether to keep it.
5. Share a plot and write a one-sentence conclusion.
