## Overview
Precipitating Change is a middle school weather forecasting unit from the Concord Consortium. Students act as scientists. They collect and analyze data, run and refine three virtual weather models, and track a virtual storm across Alaska. The unit ends with a forecast and recommendation presentation.

## Benefits for students
- Students work like real forecasters.
- Students refine models as they learn more.
- Students keep a science notebook and give a final presentation.

## Benefits for teachers
- Materials include an online teacher edition and a teacher dashboard.
- Video tutorials and a student science notebook are provided.
- A presentation template and rubric are included.

## Benefits for schools
- The unit is free per search results.
- The materials list includes items to buy, with no prices given.
- It runs in a browser, and the internet is needed at all times.

## Where is the AI?
This unit has no AI. It builds skills in data and models. Students run virtual weather models and use the results to make a forecast.

## Privacy and safety
The materials do not state whether accounts are required, and the internet is needed at all times. The unit page offers a teacher dashboard for tracking student progress. This suggests student work is stored on Concord Consortium servers, but the research did not confirm it. The materials do not say what data is collected, and the research found no privacy policy. A teacher should check the dashboard settings and the Concord privacy information.

## Ideas by subject
### science
1. Forecast a virtual storm using weather models and explain the evidence.
2. Compare the three virtual models and say which fits best.
3. Track the storm across Alaska and mark its path.
4. Refine a forecast after new data arrives.
5. Present a forecast and recommendation with the class template.

### math
1. Collect weather data and make a graph.
2. Compare two data sets and describe the difference.
3. Find the pattern in a series of pressure readings.
4. Estimate when a storm will reach a place from its speed.
5. Explain how a chart supports a forecast.
