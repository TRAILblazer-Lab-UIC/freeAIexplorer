## Overview
These are two lesson plans from Purdue's Indiana 4565 Computing Foundations for a Digital Age course. One covers privacy versus security. The other covers data privacy and consent. Students debate, sort station cards, role-play, and follow a data trail. The lessons suit grades 6-12 teachers, although the lessons do not state a grade range.

## Benefits for students
- Students stand and debate a would you rather question, which gets every voice into the room.
- Students sort real privacy and security issues at stations and learn the difference.
- Students trace a data trail and see how apps collect information about them.

## Benefits for teachers
- The lessons are a free download with printable station cards and policy excerpts.
- Each lesson ends with an exit ticket and a vocabulary dictionary.
- The role-play and station activities need very little technology.

## Benefits for schools
- The lessons cost nothing.
- Most activities run on paper. IT has little to do.
- Students can use a fictional case study, which keeps their own app settings private.

## Getting started
- [ ] Find the Purdue Indiana 4565 Computing Foundations for a Digital Age lesson PDFs and download both.
- [ ] Read Unit 2 Day 2 on privacy versus security and Unit 5 Day 6 on data privacy and consent.
- [ ] Print the station cards and the privacy policy excerpts.
- [ ] Choose a data trail option for Unit 5 Day 6.
- [ ] Pick Ava's Digital Day if you do not want students to open their own app settings.
- [ ] Pick the app permissions review only after students have an app on their own device.
- [ ] Set up the would you rather debate with one side of the room for each answer.
- [ ] Hand out the exit ticket and the vocabulary dictionary at the end.

## Where is the AI?
These lessons have no AI. They build an AI-related skill, which is thinking about data. Students see what information apps collect and who may use it. That habit helps them judge any AI tool later.

## Privacy and safety
No account is needed, and student work stays on paper or on the student's own device. The internet is used only to download the lessons. The app permissions option asks students to look at settings on a personal device. A teacher should decide whether that option fits the class, or use the fictional case study to protect student privacy.

## Ideas by subject
### computer_science
1. Debate a would you rather question about privacy and security, then explain which side felt harder to defend.
2. Sort station cards into privacy issues and security issues and defend each choice to a partner.
3. Role-play a hacker, an official, or a social media company and describe what each wants from data.
4. Read a privacy policy excerpt and underline the data that an app may collect.
5. Write a short vocabulary dictionary of privacy and security terms for a younger student.

### library_media
1. Follow Ava's Digital Day and mark each moment where a data trail appears.
2. Review the permissions of one app and list what it can reach and why that might matter.
3. Draw a map of a digital footprint and label who could see each part.
4. Read a privacy policy excerpt and write a plain-language summary for a classmate.
5. Create a poster that tells younger students how to check app permissions.
