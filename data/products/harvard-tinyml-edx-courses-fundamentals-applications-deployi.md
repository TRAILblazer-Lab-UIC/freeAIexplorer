## Overview
This is a university level series of self-paced online courses on tiny machine learning. Harvard offers it through edX. The courses cover machine learning basics, training models for keyword spotting and gesture recognition, and deploying a model to a microcontroller. They are not designed for high school. Use with advanced students is a teacher choice.

## Benefits for students
- Students learn how machine learning fits on a small chip.
- Students train models for keywords, visual wake words, and gestures.
- Students can deploy a model to a real microcontroller.

## Benefits for teachers
- The courses are self-paced, which lets teachers assign them without live teaching.
- Each of the three courses has a clear focus.
- An MLOps course sits in the same series.

## Benefits for schools
- Harvard's list says the courses are free. A search snippet lists a $299 certificate.
- An optional Arduino kit costs extra. A search snippet reports a pre-order price of $49.99 in 2020, and current prices are unknown.
- Harvard's page does not list duration or prerequisites.

## Where is the AI?
The AI is a set of small machine learning models for keyword spotting, visual wake words, and gesture recognition. Learners train them in the courses and run them on a microcontroller. The course content runs on edX in the cloud. Students extend their own work by deploying a model they trained to a board.

## Privacy and safety
Every learner needs an edX account and the internet at all times. The research did not review the edX privacy policy, and the vendor materials do not say what student data it collects. The trained models run on the learner's own microcontroller board, according to the course descriptions. The courses are university level and not designed for high school. A teacher should read the edX privacy terms and its account rules before students sign up.

## Ideas by subject
### computer_science
1. Complete the Foundations course and explain what a model is in your own words.
2. Train a keyword spotting model and test it with classmates' voices.
3. Compare the accuracy of a model before and after adding training data.
4. Describe why a small model is useful on a battery powered device.
5. Present one lesson from the Applications course to the class.

### engineering
1. Deploy a trained model to a microcontroller and test it.
2. Measure how much memory a model uses on the board.
3. Test a gesture model with a sensor and record the results.
4. Compare the speed of a small model and a larger model.
5. Write a design note about the limits of the board.
