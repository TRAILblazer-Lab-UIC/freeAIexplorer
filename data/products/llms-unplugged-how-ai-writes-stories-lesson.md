## Overview
This school lesson is for ages 10 to 16, which are years 5 to 10. It runs 60 or 90 minutes. Students generate stories from a finished paper language model and see how its tally marks were made. In the 90-minute version they train their own model on a new text. The class then pools the models into one story. Slides with presenter notes, printouts, and a lesson plan are provided.

## Benefits for students
- Students see how a language model writes stories from word counts.
- Students train a model of their own on a new text.
- Students combine class models to make one shared story.

## Benefits for teachers
- Slides come with presenter notes.
- Printouts and a lesson plan are ready to use.
- Teachers can choose the 60 or 90 minute version.

## Benefits for schools
- The materials are free.
- The lesson needs no computers and no accounts.
- ANU reported running it with about 35 Year 5 students at Duffy Primary School.

## Where is the AI?
The lesson has no running AI program. Students use a paper language model that makes text from word counts. They train a small model themselves and then pool the class models. They build skills in reading data and in seeing how text can be made by prediction.

## Privacy and safety
The lesson runs on paper. The pages read mention no devices or accounts. The research read no privacy policy for this item. The research found no student data collection. ## Ideas by subject
### computer_science
1. Roll dice to pick words from a finished paper model and write a short story.
2. Count tally marks in the model to see how word choices were made.
3. Train a model on a new short text and tally its word pairs.
4. Pool the class models and write one story from the combined counts.
5. Explain to a partner how the training text shapes the stories.

### ela
1. Compare a model story with a story written by a person.
2. Pick a favorite sentence from the model and explain why it works.
3. Edit a model story into a clear and finished piece.
4. Choose a training text from a favorite book and predict the style of the output.
5. Write a short reflection on what the model can and cannot do as a writer.
