## Overview
Open Source Physics is a collection of computational physics resources for teaching. It includes simulations, Easy Java and JavaScript Simulations for building simulations, and curriculum packages with tutorials and worksheets. It also includes the Tracker video analysis tool. Tracker Online is a JavaScript version that runs in a browser, including on Chromebooks.

## Benefits for students
- Students run physics simulations and change settings.
- Students analyze real video with Tracker.
- Students can build simulations from equations.

## Benefits for teachers
- Curriculum packages pair simulations with tutorials and student worksheets.
- The project is sponsored by the National Science Foundation and Davidson College.
- Wikipedia notes a 2011 SPORE prize, a 2015 UNESCO ICT in education prize, and a 2020 APS award.

## Benefits for schools
- The resources are free.
- Desktop tools need Java.
- Tracker Online runs in a browser.

## Getting started
- [ ] Open the ComPADRE collection.

- [ ] Try Tracker Online in a browser on Chromebooks.
- [ ] Record a short video of a moving object.
- [ ] Review a curriculum package and its worksheets.

## Where is the AI?
This collection has no AI. It builds data and modeling skills. Students track motion in video and test models against data.

## Privacy and safety
The site offers optional login and account creation. The materials do not say what data the tools collect. The ComPADRE page links Privacy and Terms pages that the research did not read. Downloaded Java simulations and Tracker run on the local computer, and the internet is used at setup for those tools. A teacher should check how student videos are stored and review the privacy of Tracker Online.

## Ideas by subject
### science
1. Film a ball toss and track it with Tracker.
2. Plot position against time and describe the motion.
3. Run a pendulum simulation and change the length.
4. Compare a simulation to a video of the same motion.
5. Complete a worksheet from a curriculum package.

### math
1. Fit a curve to the tracked data.
2. Read the equations behind a simulation.
3. Build a simple simulation from an equation in EJS.
4. Compare predicted and measured values.
5. Explain what a slope shows in a motion graph.
