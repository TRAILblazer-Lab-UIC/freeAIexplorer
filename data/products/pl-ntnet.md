## Overview
Pl@ntNet is a plant identification tool and citizen science project. It is a website and a mobile app. Users submit up to four photos of one plant and receive species suggestions across more than 70,000 species. Students photograph plants outdoors and compare the suggestions.

## Benefits for students
- Students identify a plant from a photo in seconds.
- Students can browse species galleries and regional floras.
- Students can share observations with a citizen science community.

## Benefits for teachers
- A lesson called AI Plant Classification for biology exists. It is CC BY-NC-SA 4.0.
- The tool also offers plant disease and pest identification.
- The interface comes in seven languages.

## Benefits for schools
- The tool is free to use. Pro plan pricing was not documented.
- It runs in a browser or on a phone or tablet.
- Teachers may need to install the app. Students need a camera and internet.

## Getting started
- [ ] Open identify.plantnet.org or install the app from a store.
- [ ] Ask IT to approve the app on school phones or tablets.
- [ ] Take photos of one plant, up to four.
- [ ] Pick a suggestion and read about the species.
- [ ] Create an account only for the teacher to share observations.
- [ ] Review each shared observation before it goes public.
- [ ] Compare the app suggestion with a field guide.

## Where is the AI?
The AI is an image classification model that matches plant photos to species. It runs in the vendor's cloud, which means photos are sent over the internet. Students photograph a plant, review the suggestions, and use a field guide to confirm the answer.

## Privacy and safety
An account is optional and is needed to share observations. Photos go to the vendor's cloud, and the internet is needed at all times. Shared observations, with optional location and notes, go to community review and the public Pl@ntNet database. The research did not find a privacy policy or confirm whether photos are kept or public by default. The materials do not state what student data is collected. A teacher should check photos for faces and location details before anything is shared.
