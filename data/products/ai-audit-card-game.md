## Overview
The AI Audit game is a competitive card game for middle and high school students about AI ethics. Players act as founders of AI start-ups. They challenge rivals' products by raising potential harms or defend their own with features that reduce those harms. The rules give higher rewards to ethically built systems.

## Benefits for students
- Students practice spotting harms in AI products.
- Students defend their own ideas with fair design choices.
- Students learn that ethical design can pay off in the game.

## Benefits for teachers
- The paper by Safinah Ali, Vishesh Kumar, and Cynthia Breazeal covers game design and teacher resources.
- The game needs only printed cards.
- It supports a lively class discussion on ethics.

## Benefits for schools
- The paper is free, and printing is the teacher's cost.
- The MIT page describes no software.
- No student data is collected.

## Where is the AI?
The game has no software and no AI runs on any device. Students play founders of AI start-ups and talk about harms and fixes. They build skills in thinking about the risks of AI systems and how to reduce them.

## Privacy and safety
This is a physical card game and the MIT page describes no software. The research found no student data collection. The internet is needed to download the PDF. The materials do not state whether an account is needed for that download. A teacher should read the harm cards before class. Some harms may be sensitive for some students.

## Ideas by subject
### computer_science
1. Pick an AI product idea and list the harms it could cause.
2. Design a feature that lowers one of those harms.
3. Challenge a classmate's product with a harm card and defend against a challenge.
4. Rank the harms by how likely they are and explain the order.
5. Redesign a product after a round and describe what changed.

### social_studies
1. Discuss who could be hurt by an AI product and who could gain.
2. Debate whether a company should always fix a harm even if it costs more.
3. Write a short rule that a start-up should follow to protect users.
4. Compare the game's rewards with real world incentives.
5. Present a harm to the class and suggest how a community might respond.
